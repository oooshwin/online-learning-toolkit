01: Overview

What is the APQC-PCF?

The Process Classification Framework (PCF)® is an open-standard taxonomy of cross-functional business processes designed by the American Productivity & Quality Center (APQC) and its member companies. It serves as a common language for the objective comparison of organizational performance, regardless of industry, size, or geography.

More than 80 organizations were involved in the initial design in 1992, and APQC continues to partner with over 1,000 member organizations worldwide to refine the framework. In total, the PCF organizes operating and management processes into 13 enterprise-level categories, including process groups and over 1,000 processes and associated activities.

It is still an evolving model, updated regularly to remain current with global business practices. Enhancements are driven by the development of new definitions, processes, and measures within the Open Standards Benchmarking (OSB) database.

"The PCF is the world's most widely used process framework. It gives organizations standardized definitions for functions, processes, and activities independent of business model or structure."

Core Design Principle
MECE: Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive. Every process element appears exactly once. No duplication, no gaps. This is what makes the PCF a reliable benchmarking tool.
02: Framework Structure

How the PCF is Structured

The PCF uses a hierarchical parent-child decomposition from broad categories down to granular tasks, enabling organizations to zoom in or out depending on their needs.

The Five Levels

PCF ID vs. Hierarchy ID
5-digit PCF ID: permanent, like a Social Security Number. Never changes even if you rename or restructure elements. Always retain this for benchmarking.

Hierarchy ID: (e.g., 8.1.2) flexible, like a driver's license number. Changes when you customize the framework. The PCF ID ensures the link back to APQC's research is never lost.

Cross-Industry vs. Industry-Specific

Cross-Industry
Applies to any organization. Uses general process terminology. Starting baseline for most companies. Currently at Version 8.0.
Industry-Specific
21+ versions: Banking, Retail, Airline, Healthcare, Automotive, Pharmaceutical, and more. Front-office processes differ; back-office support stays the same.
Example: MECE in Practice
An IT process used by the HR department still belongs under Category 8.0 (Manage IT). Do not duplicate it into the HR category: reference it from there. This keeps the taxonomy clean and benchmarkable across organizations.

03: IT Management Component

IT in the PCF

Category 8.0 (Manage Information Technology) is the PCF's dedicated IT category. But IT does not stop there: it acts as an enabler across every category in the framework.

Category 8.0: Process Groups
IT Strategy & Governance
Develop IT Business Strategy
Covers defining business technology and governance strategy, managing the IT portfolio, and maintaining enterprise architecture.
IT Product Development
Develop Solutions
Covers the design, building, and evaluation of IT products and services before they are released to the organization.
IT Project Management
Deploy Solutions
Covers the actual execution and rollout of IT solutions into the live organizational environment.
IT Service Management
Develop Relationships · Manage Information · Manage Support
Covers ongoing operation and maintenance of IT services after deployment: including support, information governance, and resilience.
IT Permeates Every Category
While Category 8.0 is dedicated to managing IT, IT as a function permeates every other category in the PCF. It acts as the underlying infrastructure that makes all other business processes possible and efficient.
Data & Analytics: inform decision-making across all operating categories (1-5)
ERP & CRM Systems: automate and integrate core operations in Deliver (4.0) and Market & Sell (3.0)
Collaboration Platforms: connect people across HR (6.0) and Customer Service (5.0)
Security & Compliance Tools: protect assets across Risk & Compliance (10.0)
Automation & AI: require standardized process documentation that the PCF provides before deployment
Key insight: Without IT, the processes classified in the PCF would be significantly slower, more error-prone, and harder to benchmark. IT converts the PCF's taxonomy into operational reality: allowing organizations to execute, monitor, and continuously improve the processes the framework describes.

06: Case Studies

PCF in the Real World

From Course 1 Part 2: five use cases with real organizations that deployed the PCF, each illustrating a different dimension of what the framework can do.

Use Case 1: Transformation
Organization
Elevations Credit Union
Banking PCF
In 2008, a new CEO arrived with a mandate to transform Elevations CU into a more efficient, standardized organization with the ultimate goal of winning the Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award.

The process improvement team used the Banking PCF as the foundation for a three-phase transformation spanning several years.
Phase 1: Current State Survey
Converted the Banking PCF into a department survey (Level 3). Teams rated each process 1-5 on maturity, mapping the full "as-is" state.
Phase 2: Gap Workshop
PCF sections blown up on walls; color-coded notes identified gaps. Cross-functional teams built the "to-be" map collaboratively: increasing buy-in and engagement.
Phase 3: BPM Tracking Tool
Customized PCF loaded into a BPM tool with scorecards, SIPOCs, process owners, and KPIs: enabling ongoing transformation tracking.
Won the Malcolm Baldrige Award in 2014
One of APQC's most cited case studies. Some organizations give it to new hires as required reading on their first day.
Use Case 2: Process Improvement
Organizations
Sword Group & Hewlett-Packard
Cross-Industry PCF
Two organizations used the PCF's Level 1 categories to systematically assess and prioritize improvement projects: replacing gut-feel decisions with a structured, data-driven approach across five maturity dimensions.
Sword Group: Maturity Assessment Scorecard
Scored each PCF category on Process Maturity, Business Contribution, Business Alignment, Accountability, and Performance Measurement. Plotted on a 2x2 matrix (value vs. feasibility) to prioritize projects.
HP: PCF as Project Taxonomy
Flagged all incoming improvement requests against the PCF taxonomy, identified overlapping work streams, consolidated redundant projects, and managed enterprise-wide scope.
Use Case 3: ERP / Technology Adoption
Organization
Pearson
Finance Process Standardization
Three global regions, three separate ERP implementations, three consulting teams. Pearson needed to consolidate into a single ERP, but two regional teams resisted standardization, believing their processes were unique.
PCF as Neutral Common Language
Used in cross-regional workshops as an objective third-party reference: anchoring all discussions to a shared standard and removing "our way vs. your way" dynamics.
Record-to-Report Consolidation
Two regions had 27 processes for Record-to-Report. After PCF-guided workshops, they consolidated to 21: reducing redundancy and complexity.
Outcome: Single Unified ERP Rollout
One finance process view across all three regions. A common process language made previously irreconcilable approaches alignable.
Use Case 4: Content Management
Organization
University of North Texas System
Shared Services Consolidation
Consolidating shared services (Finance, HR, Supply Chain) across multiple campuses. UNT needed to organize hundreds of scattered Standard Operating Procedures into one accessible system.
PCF as SOP Taxonomy
Used as the organizing backbone for the SOP document repository. SOPs tagged to PCF process elements in a drill-down structure: Category → Process Group → Process → SOPs.
Dual-Screen Workflow
Finance staff now work with the repository on one screen and their transaction system on the other: all procedures available in the flow of work.
Gateway to Improvement
The repository surfaced overlapping SOPs across campuses: creating visibility into improvement opportunities. A similar approach helped another organization pass an ISO audit.
Use Case 5: End-to-End & RPA
Organizations
CMI & Arla Foods
Digital Transformation
The #1 challenge in digital transformation: organizations don't have the documented, standardized process foundation needed to build automation or connect data systems. The PCF solves this prerequisite problem.
CMI: End-to-End Mapping
Used a prioritization matrix (scope, technology readiness, governance, complexity, business impact) to select processes. PCF used in day-long workshops as a completeness checklist while simultaneously identifying KPIs per step.
Arla Foods: Skipping the Documentation Phase
Had already used the PCF to standardize finance processes before their RPA project. This allowed them to skip the costly "as-is documentation" phase that stalls most automation projects.
Key Insight: Process Foundation Enables Technology
The PCF is a prerequisite for AI, RPA, and data initiatives. Organizations that standardize processes via the PCF gain a significant head start in digital transformation.
07: Strategic Alignment

PCF & the Strategic Alignment Model

Connecting the APQC-PCF to the Strategic Alignment Model (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1989): showing how a process framework bridges business and IT strategy at every level.

Henderson & Venkatraman Strategic Alignment Model (1989)
Business · External
Business Strategy
Scope, distinctive competencies, business governance
PCF: Categories 1-5 (Operating)
I/T · External
I/T Strategy
Technology scope, IT governance, distinctive competencies
PCF: Category 8.0 (Manage IT)
Business · Internal
Organizational Infrastructure
Administrative infrastructure, processes, skills
PCF: Categories 6, 7, 9-13 (Support)
I/T · Internal
I/S Infrastructure
IS infrastructure, IT processes, IT skills
PCF: Category 8.0 + cross-domain enablement
PCF covers all four SAM quadrants simultaneously: making it one of the few frameworks that functions as a cross-domain alignment instrument rather than a tool for one perspective only.
Functional Integration
PCF provides a single unified taxonomy covering both business-side (Categories 1-6) and IT/support-side (Categories 7-13) processes. Business and IT are classified, benchmarked, and improved within the same structure, not separate silos.
Strategic Integration
Category 1.0 (Develop Vision and Strategy) and Category 8.0 (Manage IT) work in tandem. Organizations define business strategy and IT strategy using the same process language, ensuring IT planning is directly informed by overall business direction.
Are Business and IT Aligned?
Yes, and the PCF is the instrument that makes that alignment visible and measurable.
PCF achieves Business-IT alignment by:
Giving both business and IT a common language to describe and discuss processes.
Making IT processes visible alongside business processes within the same framework
Enabling benchmarking of both business and IT processes against the same industry standards
Allowing organizations to identify gaps between what the business needs and what IT currently supports

09: Alternatives

Other Process Frameworks

The PCF is the most widely used, but it is not the only option. Here are two comparable frameworks and how they differ in purpose and grounding.

Value Reference Model VRM
A standardized cross-industry framework providing a unified taxonomy for classifying and benchmarking end-to-end enterprise business processes, organized around value creation logic rather than functional categories.
Similarities with PCF
Cross-industry, enterprise-wide process classification
Hierarchical, multi-level process taxonomy
Designed to support process benchmarking and improvement
Supply Chain Operations Reference SCOR-DS
A universal standard providing a unified framework for classifying, measuring, and benchmarking end-to-end supply chain processes, organized around the physical flow of goods and supply chain execution.
Similarities with PCF
Cross-industry frameworks for classifying business processes
Hierarchical, multi-level process taxonomy
Standard benchmarking metrics and performance measures
Framework Comparison
Dimension PCF VRM SCOR-DS
Primary LensFunctional categories (what organizations do)Value creation logic (how value is generated)Physical flow of goods (supply chain execution)
GroundingPragmatically grounded in how organizations actually operateTheoretically grounded in value chain thinkingOperationally grounded in supply chain execution

Best ForEnterprise-wide benchmarking, process discovery, and IT alignment across all functionsValue chain analysis and value-driven improvementComplex supply chains needing deep operational benchmarking
